Since the model is the main product, I'd show it off through things that make its reasoning visible, not through multiplayer engineering. Real-time rooms, lobbies and syncing game state between players are a lot of work, and none of it shows off the model. Here's what I'd suggest, strongest first.

1. Investigator dashboard (the core demo)

This is a web app built on held-out data that looks like a fraud analyst's tool:

  • Ranked alert list: pairs sorted by risk score, with filters by predicted behavior (directed transfer, soft play, coordinated isolation, other).
  • Pair detail page: the top 5 evidence hands, which features drove the score (SHAP), and stats over time showing when the coordination was active.
  • Hand replayer: an animated table that plays each evidence hand action by action and highlights the suspicious decision (e.g., "folded top set to partner's river bet").
  • "Benign alternative" panel: a plausible innocent explanation for each alert, such as tilt or a weak player. The competition itself asked for this in its case reviews, and it shows you understand false positives.

One catch: the evaluation labels are private. So the dashboard should either use a held-out slice of the labeled development pairs, where you can show ground truth next to the prediction, or show evaluation-set predictions next to your Kaggle late-submission score.

2. "Beat the detector" mode (replaces the poker rooms)

You play 6-max against bots, and one bot is your secret partner. You try to collude (dumping chips, soft-playing each other) without getting caught, and a live risk meter reacts as hands go by. It's still playable poker, but now its job is to demonstrate the model. It's single-player against bots, which avoids the multiplayer complexity and the data-sparsity problem. It's also memorable for anyone viewing your portfolio.

The caveat: you'd write the bots' collusion rules yourself, not Kaggle's generator, so this doubles as an honest test of how well the model generalizes. If it misses things, that's worth writing up rather than hiding.

3. Collusion simulator sandbox

Sliders for collusion type, how often the pair coordinates (it's episodic) and how blatant it is. Run 1,000 hands of bots and plot how quickly the risk score separates colluders from innocent players. This shows detection latency and sensitivity, which interviewers tend to ask about.

4. Results and model card page (the ML rigor)

  • Kaggle late-submission score, broken down into Pair AP, Evidence MAP@5 and Behavior MAP.
  • Precision-recall curves per behavior, ablations (what each feature group adds), and error analysis of the decoys like tilt and repeated opponent selection.
  • A model card covering the synthetic-data limitations and the domain shift to real poker.

5. Scoring API

A documented /score endpoint: upload hand histories in the dataset schema and get back flagged pairs plus evidence. It shows the model is deployable, not just a notebook.

My recommendation

Build 1 + 4 as the must-haves, which is the model story. Then add 2 as the interactive hook in place of multiplayer rooms. Multiplayer rooms can come later if you still want them, and the hand logging and model service would carry over unchanged.

Why the detector is allowed to see all the cards

  • This dataset reveals everyone's hole cards, including folded hands, in seats.parquet. You've seen this in the hand printouts: every seat shows its two cards.
  • Real poker sites work the same way. The server deals the cards, so it logs every hole card. Fraud teams review hands with this full view. The model plays the role of that fraud team, not of a player.
  • In our "Beat the Detector" game the detector will also see all cards, because it's the house. The bots playing against you won't.

Why seeing hidden cards is exactly what catches collusion

The key is how hidden cards are used: not to judge whether a decision was good, but to test whether decisions depend on information the player shouldn't have.

  • An honest player can't see the opponent's cards. Whether they fold is unrelated to whether they were actually ahead, beyond what the board and betting reveal. Over many hands, an honest player folds while secretly ahead at about the same rate against everyone.
  • A colluder often does know their partner's cards, because sharing hole cards is the classic form of collusion. Or they're simply following a plan to give chips away. Either way, their folds line up with the hidden cards: they fold while ahead much more often against the partner than against anyone else.

So a single "folded while ahead" proves nothing. Honest players do it all the time, for example when a bluff works. The lift design from P2 compares each player with themselves: the rate against the partner minus the rate against others. A pattern that only appears against one specific opponent means the decisions carry information they shouldn't.
