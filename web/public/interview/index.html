Data and ingest

S1 · ingest.py

The starting point is Kaggle's Detect Suspicious Value Transfers in Poker: 2,000,000 synthetic six-max No-Limit Hold'em hands. They come as 12M seat rows, 18.6M actions and 12,000 players at 400 tables of 30. Only 1,860 pairs are labeled (372 colluding), and 112,540 evaluation pairs must be scored. Ingest reads the raw parquet files, types every column, parses cards into integers, and maps string IDs to integer codes. Everything downstream works on these tables.

Why it is built this way

  • Semantics were proven, not assumed. Before writing features, I checked what amount, amount_to, to_call and pot_before mean with invariant checks on 400,000 hands (20% of the data). Blinds are not actions, uncalled bets stay in the pot, and there is no rake. The result is a written rule book that the game's hand logger must also follow.
  • Integer IDs. A full-data check keyed on string IDs ran for over 30 minutes. Integer codes make joins and group-bys several times faster. The original strings are restored only for the submission, because the metric breaks ties by the string pair_id.
  • Polars. It's multithreaded and columnar, so 18.6M actions fit in 16 GB of RAM. The whole pipeline runs in 7.3 minutes with a 6.9 GB peak, reached during this stage.
  • Rules first. The player metadata table is never a feature: the rules say coordination must be inferred from gameplay only.

Top 10 interview questions

Q1Describe the dataset.

2M hands in four tables: hands (table, time, phase, button, blinds, board, pot), seats (12M rows: player, seat, stack, hole cards, contribution, net chips, folded, showdown, won share), actions (18.6M rows: street, player, action, amount, pot before, stack before, to call, players active) and players (metadata). Labels: 1,860 development pairs with a scheme, 1,817 planted evidence hands, and 112,540 evaluation pairs to score. Every seat's hole cards are known, even for folded hands.

Q2Why convert string IDs to integers?

Speed and memory. Joins, sorts and group-bys on integers are much cheaper than on strings, and a string-keyed full-data check took over 30 minutes. The catch: the metric breaks ties by the string pair_id, so integer codes are internal only, and every submission is mapped back to the original strings.

Q3How did you establish what each column means?

With invariants, run as a script over 20% of the data. For example: pot_before[i+1] = pot_before[i] + amount[i], net chips sum to zero per hand (no rake), and to_call = min(highest commitment − own commitment, stack). Each rule held for 100% of rows, and the rules went into a document that the simulator's logger must obey. Later, a 34-invariant conformance test encoded the same rules.

Q4Why Polars instead of pandas?

Pandas is single-threaded and memory-hungry with strings, and the action table has 18.6M rows on a 16 GB laptop. Polars is multithreaded, stores data in Arrow columns and has a lazy query engine. One catch I had to handle: Polars group_by and join don't guarantee row order, which broke reproducible sampling until I sorted before sampling.

Q5Why is the player metadata table excluded?

The rules require inferring coordination from gameplay only: no ID formats, row order or generator artifacts. Metadata like region or account age could encode how the generator picked colluders, a shortcut that wouldn't work on a real site. It would also be unfair to flag someone for their region.

Q6How do you keep time order correct?

Only from hands.started_at, never from IDs, integer codes or row order. Row order is an artifact of how the files were written, and the rules forbid using it. Time order matters for the episodic block features and the 2,000-hand windows.

Q7Why are all money features in big blinds?

Stakes are fixed per table (big blind 2, 4 or 10). A 100-chip pot is huge at one table and small at another. Dividing by the big blind makes features comparable across tables, and rates per 100 hands make them comparable across pairs with different exposure.

Q8What's tricky about uncalled bets?

The data doesn't return an uncalled final bet as a separate step. It stays in total_contribution and final_pot, and the winner's net chips cancel it out. So "how much was really contested" has to subtract the uncalled part: the last aggressor's commitment above the second-highest. Getting this wrong inflates the chip flow between players.

Q9How do the same features run on simulated and live data?

Ingest exposes one function that takes any tables in the competition schema. The simulator and the live game write hands in exactly that schema, so the same code path (encode, then features, then models) scores all three sources. One feature module for everything is an architecture rule of the project.

Q10How much memory and time does the pipeline need?

End to end, raw data to models: 7.3 minutes with a 6.9 GB peak on a 16-thread laptop, against limits of 45 minutes and 12 GB. Ingest takes 21 seconds and is the memory peak. Stages run one at a time and cache their outputs as parquet, so a later stage can be rerun alone.

Hand strength and exact odds

S2 / S2E · cards.py, equity.py

This component measures how strong each player's hand was at every decision. Preflop, that's the percentile of the 169 starting-hand classes; postflop, the made-hand rank of the best five cards. It also computes the responder's exact odds of winning (equity) at each of 8.47M decisions where one player faces another's bet. From the equity it gets the expected value of calling, so a fold or call can be priced in big blinds.

Why it is built this way

  • The auditor's view. All hole cards are revealed after the hand, as a site's fraud team would see them. Honest decisions can't depend on cards the player can't see. A colluder who knows the partner's cards (or plays to a plan) shows a different pattern against that partner.
  • phevaluator. A C extension that does 0.96M evaluations per second, 10× faster than the pure-Python treys, with identical ranks (1 = royal flush … 7,462 = worst). It also has a Linux wheel for the server.
  • No look-ahead. Future board cards stay in the deck. Equity is what the player's hand was worth at that moment, not what happened later.
  • Precision only where it pays. Preflop uses a precomputed 169×169 heads-up table. The flop uses Monte Carlo over 100 runouts. The turn and river are enumerated exactly, because few cards remain.

Top 10 interview questions

Q1Why do you need hand strength at all?

Collusion shows up as decisions that make no sense given the cards: folding the best hand to the partner's bet, or calling the partner with junk. Without strength you can only see actions, and an action like "fold" is normal most of the time. Strength turns "fold" into "folded while ahead", which is rare for honest players.

Q2How is strength computed preflop and postflop?

Preflop: each hand's percentile among the 169 starting-hand classes, from an equity table (each class against a random hand, 20,000 Monte Carlo trials, fixed seed). Postflop: phevaluator's rank of the best five cards from hole cards plus board. Because every player's cards are known, "ahead of the partner" is just a comparison of two ranks.

Q3Why phevaluator over treys or your own lookup?

I benchmarked 200,000 random seven-card hands: phevaluator 0.96M/s, treys 0.09M/s, identical ranks. At that speed a custom lookup wasn't worth writing. Its card IDs (rank × 4 + suit) became the project's internal card encoding.

Q4What is equity, and how do you compute it?

Equity is the probability of winning the pot (plus half of ties) if all remaining cards were dealt. Preflop it comes from a symmetric 169×169 heads-up table built once. On the flop it's Monte Carlo over 100 random turn-river runouts. On the turn and river I enumerate every remaining card exactly.

Q5What does "no look-ahead" mean here, and why does it matter?

When computing equity at a decision, the cards that came later in the real hand are put back in the deck. Otherwise a player who folded a hand that later lost would look clever, and the model would learn from information nobody had at decision time. That's leakage in time.

Q6How do you turn equity into a cost?

The call's expected value is equity × (pot + price) − price. If folding throws away at least 1 big blind of expected value, it's a "costly fold"; a call that loses at least 1 bb in expectation is a "costly call". Summed per pair and compared with each player's own rate, these become the EV-gift lifts.

Q7Did the exact-odds features help?

Modestly: the 3-seed local score rose +0.005 (all three seeds better), but Kaggle stayed flat within noise. Evidence ranking didn't improve, because the simpler "folded while ahead" flag already separated planted hands better (10–13× vs 4–9×). The precise version mostly duplicated it for single hands, and helped a little when summed over many hands.

Q8Isn't it unrealistic to use other players' hidden cards?

Not for this use. The model isn't a player; it's an auditor reviewing finished hands, like a site's integrity team, who do see all cards afterwards. The point is precisely that honest players can't react to hidden cards, so any relationship-specific dependence on them is suspicious.

Q9How did you make 8.47M equity calculations fast enough?

Three things: lookup tables for preflop, exact enumeration only where it's cheap (turn and river), and a process pool across 15 workers for the flop Monte Carlo. The result is cached as a parquet file (about 98 seconds to build), so feature work doesn't recompute it. The live game caches equity per hand, so a rescore only computes the newest hand.

Q10How do you test an equity calculator?

Against cases with known answers. Unit tests check that turn equity matches an exact count over the river cards, that river equity equals the actual showdown result, and that the preflop table is consistent (A's equity against B plus B's against A is 1). The strength-at-decision logic has its own tests on hand-crafted hands.

Event tables and player baselines

S3 / S4 · interactions.py, baselines.py

Before any features exist, the pipeline builds four reusable event tables from the raw actions:

  • response events: player X answering player Y's bet;
  • pairwise chip flows: chips moving between each pair in each hand;
  • heads-up streets: streets with only the two players left;
  • seat pairs: who sat where, including "step-asides" (folding right after a specific player entered).

Separately, player baselines summarise each player's own style: how often they enter pots (VPIP), raise preflop (PFR), how aggressive they are, how often they fold to a bet or reach showdown, and how they play after a big loss.

Why it is built this way

  • Events first, features second. The heavy logic (who was the aggressor, who answered whom) lives in one place. Pair features are then cheap aggregations. The same event code runs on Kaggle data, the simulator and live games.
  • Exact multiway accounting. The flow from A to B in a hand is contribution(A) × won_share(B), which splits multiway and split pots correctly.
  • Baselines are the decoy defence. A player's own style is the comparison point for every relationship feature, and it is also given to the model directly so it can say "these are just two loose players".

Top 10 interview questions

Q1What is an event table, and why build them?

A table where each row is one meaningful interaction, such as "X faced Y's raise on the turn and folded while ahead". Features are then group-bys over events. That separates hard poker logic from simple aggregation, keeps the logic testable in one place, and lets the same code serve training, the simulator and the live game.

Q2How do you compute chips moving between two players in a multiway pot?

flow(A→B) = contribution(A) × won_share(B): of everything A put in, B receives B's share of the pot. Summed over hands, it gives the net flow between every pair, even in four-way pots and split pots. It's exact, with no attribution guesswork.

Q3How do you decide who is the "aggressor" a player is responding to?

The last player to bet or raise on the current street. Edge cases are unit-tested on hand-crafted hands: preflop limps facing only the big blind count as unopened, and an all-in that doesn't exceed the amount to call is a call, not a raise (about 52% of all-ins are calls).

Q4How did you verify the event tables are correct?

Against official numbers: the shared-hand count my code computes equals shared_hands in the evaluation file for all 112,540 pairs. That's a unit test. And the candidate-hand filter keeps all 1,817 planted evidence hands, so no labeled signal is lost.

Q5What is a candidate hand, and why filter to them?

A shared hand where the two players actually interacted: both put chips in voluntarily, one answered the other's bet, or one stepped aside after the other entered. It keeps 100% of the planted evidence hands while removing about 60% of shared hands. That's less noise for the hand-level models and less compute.

Q6Which player baselines do you compute, and why?

VPIP, PFR, aggression factor, fold/call/raise rate facing a bet, betting rate when checked to, went-to-showdown rate, overall win rate, and extra pots entered right after a big loss (tilt). They're the denominators of every lift ("rate against everyone else") and the control features that let the model explain away style.

Q7What is a step-aside, and what did it show?

Folding preflop right after the partner entered, compared with folding after anyone else entered. I expected squeeze partners to step aside more. The data showed the opposite: colluders step aside less (they want to be in pots together), and the feature is strongly predictive in that direction (single-feature AP 0.82). I kept it with the corrected explanation.

Q8How do you handle episodic coordination?

Colluders don't cheat in every hand, and may be active only part of the time. Key rates are recomputed per block of 500 table-hands, and each pair gets the most extreme block and the share of blocks with a raised rate. Blocks are much cheaper than rolling windows over ~30M pair-hand rows, and capture the same idea.

Q9Why compare a player with their own baseline and not with the population?

Population comparisons flag style: loose players call more than average against everyone. Comparing with the player's own rate against everyone else isolates what is special about one opponent. That's the core idea of the project.

Q10How fast is this stage?

The pair features stage, including the event tables, takes under a minute in the final pipeline (48 seconds), down from about 2.5 minutes early on, thanks to integer IDs and Polars expressions. The live game runs the same code on a few hundred hands in a fraction of a second per rescore on a full CPU core.

Pair features: the lift principle

S5 / S5W · features.py, pipeline.py

This stage turns the event tables into one row per pair, with 88 features in eight families:

  • exposure;
  • chip flow;
  • directed-transfer signals;
  • soft-play signals;
  • isolation signals;
  • episodic (time-block) signals;
  • player-style controls;
  • presence (play with the partner seated vs not).

Almost every relationship feature is a lift: how a player treats the partner, minus how they treat everyone else, shrunk toward zero when there's little data. The S5W stage recomputes the same features on 2,000-hand windows, so training data matches the evaluation period's exposure.

Why it is built this way

  • Lift beats raw rates. Raw call rate scores AP 0.10 for chip dumping; the call lift scores 0.96. Loose, tilting and weak players behave the same against everyone, so their lift is about 0.
  • Shrinkage stops small samples from shouting. shrunk = (events vs Y + k·base) / (opportunities vs Y + k) with k = 20; lift = shrunk − base.
  • Symmetric by max/min. The label doesn't say who gives and who receives, so each directional feature becomes "the more extreme partner" and "the other".
  • Matched exposure. Evaluation pairs share about 40% fewer hands. Rates, period shares and window training views keep train and test comparable.

Top 10 interview questions

Q1Explain the lift principle simply.

Don't ask "does this player call a lot?" Ask "does this player call this opponent more than everyone else?" A loose player is loose against everyone, so nothing stands out. A colluder treats one person specially. Raw call rate gives AP 0.10 for chip dumping; the call lift gives 0.96.

Q2What is the shrinkage, statistically?

Empirical Bayes with a Beta-Binomial model. The rate against the partner gets a Beta prior centred on the player's own rate against everyone else, worth k = 20 pseudo-observations. The posterior mean is (x + k·base)/(n + k). With 3 opportunities the prior dominates; with 300 the data does. It's the same reason a product with one 5-star review shouldn't outrank one with 2,000 reviews averaging 4.8.

Q3Why a difference and not a ratio?

A ratio explodes when the baseline is tiny (1% vs 3% looks like "3×") and is undefined at 0. A difference of shrunk rates is bounded, stable and in natural units ("calls 25 percentage points more often against this opponent").

Q4Why take max and min over the two directions?

The pair is unordered and the label doesn't say who gives and who receives. Separate "player 1" and "player 2" columns would be arbitrary, and the model would have to learn everything twice. Max/min is invariant to listing order and still keeps the asymmetry ("one partner does this a lot").

Q5Walk me through the feature families.
  • Exposure: shared, contested and heads-up hands, as rates.
  • Chip flow: net bb/100, asymmetry, how concentrated one player's losses are on the partner.
  • Directed transfer: folding while ahead, junk calls, paying off while behind, EV given away.
  • Soft play: fewer raises, checking strong hands down heads-up.
  • Isolation: squeezes, third players pushed out.
  • Episodic: the most extreme 500-hand block.
  • Controls: each player's own style.
  • Presence: VPIP and opens with the partner seated vs not.
  • Hand suspicion: summaries of a hand-level model; see the next component.
Q6Which features matter most?

By mean |SHAP|: presence 22%, controls 20%, hand suspicion 19%, isolation 15%. In a drop-one-family ablation, removing presence cost the most window AP (−0.127), then directed transfer (−0.020) and isolation (−0.017). Removing raw exposure features slightly helped (+0.005), because exposure was controlled by the generator: shared-hand count alone scores AP equal to the base rate.

Q7Why train on 2,000-hand windows?

Adversarial validation (a classifier asked to tell evaluation pairs from development pairs) reached AUC 0.95 on full-period features, a big distribution shift. On features recomputed from 2,000-hand windows, AUC dropped to 0.67. A model trained on full-period features lost 0.07 AP on windows; training on windows recovered +0.026. So every development pair is used in three views: the full period plus two windows, all in the same fold.

Q8How were the presence features discovered?

From error analysis: isolation pairs were caught far less often (mirror AP 0.13 vs about 0.5 for the other schemes). Reading missed cases showed one partner opening weak hands more when the other was merely seated, a signal no feature measured. Presence compares a player's VPIP, PFR and weak opens with vs without the partner at the table. Isolation AP rose 0.13 → 0.30, overall 0.62 → 0.70.

Q9Why are controls important if they're not collusion signals?

They're context. A high call lift means something different for a tight player than for a calling station. Among the riskiest 1% of pairs, controls push the risk down for 34% of them, i.e. they're the model's way of saying "this is mostly style".

Q10Tell me about a feature hypothesis that was wrong.

Step-aside: I expected squeeze partners to fold more once the partner entered. The data said colluders fold less (they want to share pots), most of all in chip dumping and soft play. The feature is strong (AP 0.82) in the opposite direction. I kept it and corrected the explanation: measure, then update the story.

Validation, metric and submission

metric.py, train.py, validate_submission.py

This component decides whether any change is real. It has four parts:

  • A local copy of the official metric: 70% Pair AP + 20% Evidence MAP@5 + 10% Behavior MAP. A parity test checks it against the official code to 1e-12.
  • Out-of-fold scores: from 5-fold StratifiedGroupKFold, grouped by table and stratified by scheme.
  • Three validation sets: labeled, mirror and window.
  • A submission validator that every Kaggle file must pass.

Why it is built this way

  • Base rate matters for AP. The labeled set is 20% positive and gives AP ≈ 0.99, which says nothing. The mirror set adds every unlabeled pair that passes the organiser's own evaluation filter (122,580 pairs, 0.30% positive). The window set also matches evaluation exposure, and it is the leaderboard proxy.
  • Group by table. Players belong to one table, and colluders repeat across pairs. A random split would let the model memorise players.
  • Noise floor. Every experiment runs under three fold seeds. The spread (±0.004) is the bar a change must clear.
  • Submission rule. Submit to Kaggle only after a local 3-seed improvement, so the leaderboard never becomes the validation set.

Top 10 interview questions

Q1Explain the competition metric.

0.70 × Pair AP + 0.20 × Evidence MAP@5 + 0.10 × Behavior MAP:

  • Pair AP: average precision of the risk ranking, with ties broken by the string pair_id.
  • Evidence MAP@5: for each truly colluding pair, the AP@5 of my five hands against the planted ones.
  • Behavior MAP: a one-vs-rest AP per scheme, where a pair's score for a scheme is its risk if I predicted that scheme, else 0.

The weights weren't in the description; I found them in the official metric notebook and ported it.

Q2How do you know your metric code matches Kaggle's?

A parity test runs my port and the official code on 25 random submissions with deliberate ties, and requires agreement to 1e-12. An audit also checked it line by line: the AP formula, tie-breaking, the AP@5 denominator min(planted, 5), and macro-averaging over the three disclosed schemes.

Q3Why StratifiedGroupKFold, grouped by table?

Each player belongs to exactly one table, and some colluders appear in 2–3 positive pairs. A random split puts the same player in train and validation, so the model memorises players, not behaviour. Grouping by table mimics evaluation, which has unseen players. Stratifying by scheme keeps every fold supplied with the rarest scheme (92 isolation pairs) and mirrors the leaderboard's stratified split.

Q4Your labeled-set AP is 0.99. Why don't you report it?

AP depends on the base rate. At 20% positives, even a mediocre ranking has high precision at the top. The real task has about 0.3% positives, so I built the mirror set (realistic base rate) and the window set (realistic base rate and exposure). Confirmed negatives also turned out to be ordinary pairs, not hard ones (a classifier separates them from random pairs with AUC only 0.62).

Q5Why is the local score (0.656) far below Kaggle (0.836)?

The window proxy counts every unlabeled pair as innocent, but the organisers said the labels don't list every colluder. Treating just the 40 unlabeled pairs with out-of-fold risk above 0.99 as colluders lifts window Pair AP from 0.655 to 0.791; with the 103 above 0.9 it reaches 0.852. The proxy is for direction (did a change help?), not for the absolute level.

Q6How do you know an improvement is real?

Every change runs under three fold seeds and is logged as mean ± spread. The spread on the window final is about ±0.004, so smaller changes are noise. A change counts only if the mean moves clearly and all three seeds agree. Hand-suspicion features passed (+0.023, all seeds); hyperparameter variants didn't (±0.003).

Q7What leakage did you guard against?
  • Players shared across folds → group by table.
  • Time and ID leakage → time order from started_at only.
  • Period leakage → evaluation features and evidence use evaluation-period hands only.
  • Stacking leakage → any model whose output feeds another model is out of fold.
  • Sampling leakage → unlabeled negatives come from training folds only, and a pair's three views share a fold.
  • One known second-order leak (the negative-cleaning first pass) is documented and accepted, as in standard stacking.
Q8What does the submission validator check?

Row count, exact column order, no empty cells, no repeated hands, every evidence hand from the evaluation period and containing both players, every score in [0, 1], and all three schemes present (a missing scheme would collapse that scheme's AP). An audit found that a harmless tied-score warning had been skipping the evidence check; I fixed it and added a regression test.

Q9When do you predict "none" for behaviour?

Almost never. A pair predicted as scheme X sits in X's list ordered by risk; every other pair sits in a tie pile at 0 in effectively random order. Moving low-risk pairs to "none" only swaps a risk-ordered tail for a random one, so it helps only if that tail is worse than random. Measured gain: ≤ 0.0013, so the rule is to adopt a threshold only if it gains > 0.005, and none did.

Q10What was your rule for Kaggle submissions, and why?

Submit only when the local 3-seed window score improved. It keeps the public leaderboard from becoming a tuning target and turns each submission into a test of one hypothesis. I used 4 of a 10-submission budget, each logged with its commit, the change, the local score and both Kaggle scores.

Risk, behaviour and novelty models (M1–M3)

S6 · modeling.py, train.py
  • M1 risk: a LightGBM binary classifier, three seeds averaged, Platt-calibrated. It ranks every pair by how likely they are colluding.
  • M2 behaviour: a LightGBM multiclass model trained on colluding pairs only. It answers "if they collude, which scheme?"
  • M3 novelty: an Isolation Forest on the lift and presence features, to flag unusual pairs as other_coordination.
  • Baselines B0 and B1 ("most shared hands", "largest net chip flow") prove the model learned something simple rules can't.

Why it is built this way

  • Gradient-boosted trees for small tabular data. 1,860 labeled pairs (372 positive) is far too few for deep learning. Trees handle mixed scales and missing values, and give exact SHAP explanations.
  • Conservative settings, no tuning search: learning rate 0.02, 15 leaves, 500 trees, 60% of features and 80% of rows per tree, L2 = 1. Five variants all landed within ±0.003, which is noise.
  • Unlabeled pairs as noisy negatives. 5,000 per training view taught the model what "normal" looks like at scale (mirror AP 0.591 → 0.621). A first pass then removes the ~190 most suspicious ones as likely hidden colluders.
  • Calibration. Platt scaling keeps the ranking unchanged but makes risk usable as a probability in the live game.

Top 10 interview questions

Q1Why LightGBM and not deep learning?
  • Data size: 372 positive pairs.
  • Data shape: engineered tabular features, where boosted trees are still the strongest default.
  • Explainability: exact TreeSHAP for investigators.
  • Iteration speed: a 3-seed out-of-fold run takes about 6 minutes on a laptop.

A sequence model over actions or a graph model over the player network is a planned comparison, not the starting point.

Q2Walk me through M1's hyperparameters.

Learning rate 0.02, 15 leaves, 500 trees, at least 20 rows per leaf, 60% of features and 80% of rows per tree, L2 penalty 1.0. Everything is chosen to reduce variance on 372 positives: small trees, slow learning, row and column subsampling, regularisation. M2 is even smaller (7 leaves, 300 trees) because it sees positives only.

Q3Did you tune hyperparameters?

I tested five M1 variants (bigger trees, smaller trees with more rounds, a lower learning rate, more unlabeled negatives). All were within ±0.003 window AP, inside the noise. So I kept the defaults. Gains came from features and data, not tuning, and a big search on 372 positives would mostly have fitted noise.

Q4Why average three seeds?

LightGBM's row and feature subsampling are random, so one model has seed variance. Averaging three seeds is a cheap ensemble that lowers variance without changing bias, and makes results reproducible.

Q5Why add unlabeled pairs as negatives? Isn't that label noise?

Yes, deliberately a little. Confirmed negatives are only 1,488 ordinary pairs, while the real population is ~120,000 pairs, almost all innocent. Adding 5,000 unlabeled pairs per view (from training folds only) teaches normal variation at scale; hidden colluders among them are under 1%. Mirror AP rose 0.591 → 0.621. This is the naive form of positive-unlabeled (PU) learning.

Q6What is "cleaned negatives"?

A first M1 pass scores the unlabeled pairs out of fold. The ~190 above 0.5, which are likely hidden colluders, are removed from the negatives for the final fit. It's the "reliable negatives" step of PU learning. The window proxy can't reward it (it still counts hidden colluders as negatives), but it was neutral there and improved the mirror set (+0.007) with lower variance.

Q7What does calibration change?

M1 trains at a much higher positive rate than reality, so its raw probabilities are too high. Platt scaling fits sigmoid(a·logit(raw) + b) on the window set at a realistic base rate. It's monotone, so ranking and AP are unchanged. It matters in the game, where fixed thresholds are applied and risk is shown as a percentage.

Q8How is the scheme label chosen?

argmax of M2 over the three disclosed schemes for every pair, because a wrong scheme only hurts one scheme's AP while "none" scores zero everywhere. other_coordination is assigned only when M3's novelty is at the 99th percentile or above, M2 is unsure (top probability below 0.5) and risk is above the median.

Q9How does M3 (Isolation Forest) work, and is it worth it?

It builds 200 random trees by splitting on random features at random values. Unusual pairs get isolated in fewer splits, so a short average path means "novel". It needs no labels, which suits a scheme nobody labeled. To be fair: it changes the score by under 0.001. It exists to cover the fourth label and unknown schemes.

Q10What do the baselines show?

On the window set: B0 (most shared hands) 0.015, B1 (largest net chip flow, evidence = biggest pots won) 0.058, SusPoker 0.656. B1 shows that "follow the money" alone fails: winning and losing chips is what poker is, so the signal is in how chips move, relative to each player's normal play.

Hand suspicion (H) and evidence ranker (M4)

S5H / S6 · suspicion.py, train.py

Two hand-level LightGBM models.

  • H scores every shared hand for "looks planted". Each pair then gets summaries of its most suspicious hands (hs_max, mean of the top 3 and top 5, the count above 0.5, and that count's share), which feed M1 and M2.
  • M4 ranks a colluding pair's candidate hands to choose the five evidence hands an investigator should open first.

Why it is built this way

  • A few hands carry the signal. A colluding pair shares 100–200 hands, but only a handful are planted. Pair features average over all of them, which dilutes the signal. H finds the needles. It's multiple-instance learning: a pair is a bag of hands, and hs_max is max-pooling.
  • Clean negatives for H. Positives are planted hands. Negatives come from confirmed-innocent pairs only, because a colluding pair's unlisted hands may be planted too.
  • M4 learns the within-pair order. Evidence MAP only scores truly colluding pairs, so M4 trains on those pairs' hands only. It sees per-hand behaviour, the hand's rank within the pair, and the predicted scheme.
  • Read the misses. Evidence jumped 0.415 → 0.472 locally, and +0.039 on Kaggle, after reading the planted hands M4 ranked lowest: many planted actions were preflop, which the postflop-only logic couldn't see.

Top 10 interview questions

Q1Why score individual hands at all?

Because pair features average everything. If 5 of 150 shared hands are planted, their signal is diluted 30-fold, worst of all at the evaluation period's lower exposure. Scoring hands and keeping the top few lets those hands speak. It gave +0.023 locally (all three seeds); together with cleaned negatives, +0.0145 on Kaggle private.

Q2What's multiple-instance learning, and how does it apply?

Training data comes as labeled bags of instances: a bag is positive if at least one instance is. Here a pair is a bag of hands, positive if some hands are planted. Scoring instances and pooling (max, top-3 mean) is the classic approach, and that's exactly what the hs_* features are.

Q3How do you stop H's output leaking into M1?

H is trained out of fold by table: for each fold, H learns from the other folds' labeled pairs and scores this fold's hands. So no pair's features come from a model that saw its own table's labels. That's standard stacking discipline.

Q4Why are colluding pairs' non-evidence hands excluded from H's negatives?

Coordination is episodic, and only about 4.9 hands per pair are listed as evidence. Other hands of the same pair may also be manipulated. Labeling them "clean" would teach H that planted-looking hands are normal, so negatives come only from confirmed-innocent pairs.

Q5How does M4 pick the five evidence hands?

It scores every candidate hand (where the two players actually interacted). It uses 50 features: per-hand versions of the pair signals (fold while ahead, junk calls, strong check-downs, squeezes, weak opens, EV given away), the hand's pot and flow rank within the pair, the pair's behaviour in the same time block, and M2's predicted scheme. The top five are submitted, and NO_EVIDENCE pads pairs with fewer candidates.

Q6Why train M4 only on colluding pairs' hands?

Evidence MAP only counts truly colluding pairs, so the only thing that matters is the order within such a pair. Innocent pairs' hands would teach "this pair is innocent", which is M1's job, and would swamp the training set with irrelevant negatives.

Q7Is M4 classification or ranking?

It's pointwise learning-to-rank: a binary classifier whose scores are used to sort. A pairwise or listwise objective (LightGBM's LambdaRank grouped by pair) optimises the within-pair order directly, and is the natural next experiment, since evidence is where the gap to the top teams is (about 0.47 vs 0.76).

Q8How did the preflop signatures come about?

I read, locally, the planted hands M4 ranked lowest, next to the unplanted hands it ranked highest. Planted chip-dumping and soft-play actions were often preflop: folding a clearly better starting hand to the partner's raise, or calling the partner's raise with junk. The ahead/behind logic was postflop-only, so these were invisible. Adding them raised evidence 0.415 → 0.472, and Kaggle +0.039.

Q9Which scheme has the weakest evidence, and why?

Isolation: AP@5 0.18, vs 0.53 for chip dumping and 0.60 for soft play. Its defining move (one partner opens, the other steps aside) is barely more common on planted hands than on the pair's other hands (lift ≈ 1.6, vs ≈ 10 for folding while ahead). Squeezes also look like two aggressive players.

Q10Why do evidence improvements transfer to Kaggle better than pair-ranking ones?

Evidence is measured exactly offline: the planted hands are known, so the local evidence score is a faithful copy of Kaggle's. Pair AP locally is measured against incomplete labels. Evidence gains transferred at about 2.4× the local gain, pair-ranking gains at only about 0.35–0.6×. Trust a proxy in proportion to how faithfully it copies the target.

Explainability

explain.py

Every alert comes with three things:

  • Reasons: exact SHAP attributions from M1 (and M2 for the predicted scheme), shown as the top five reasons for and against, in plain English.
  • Reason tags: 1–3 short labels on each evidence hand, such as "Folded the better hand to the partner's bet after the flop".
  • An innocent explanation: the most plausible innocent story, chosen from 8 templates (passive player, losing player, tilt, aggressive player, similar styles, schedule overlap, small sample, neighbours).

Why it is built this way

  • A score is a prompt for review, not a verdict. An investigator needs to know why and where to look, and a false accusation bans an innocent customer.
  • Exact, not approximate. SHAP values come from LightGBM's built-in TreeSHAP, averaged over the three seed models. They add up to every raw score within ~1e-14 on all 112,540 evaluation pairs.
  • Both directions. Among the riskiest 1% of pairs, style controls push risk down for 34%. Showing only upward reasons would overstate every case.
  • Measured, not intuited. Tag order comes from measured lift on planted hands, and templates fire only when their trigger is in the most extreme 10% of a reference population.

Top 10 interview questions

Q1How do you explain a single risk score?

With exact TreeSHAP values from M1, in log-odds, averaged over the three seed models. Each pair shows its five biggest upward and five biggest downward contributions, translated into plain English via a feature catalogue. Base value plus contributions equals the model's raw score exactly.

Q2What are Shapley values?

A fair way to split a payout among cooperating players, from game theory. For a model, the players are features and the payout is "this prediction minus the average prediction". A feature's value is its average marginal contribution over all orders of adding features. It's the unique attribution with efficiency (sums to the total), symmetry, dummy (unused features get 0) and additivity.

Q3How did you verify the SHAP values?

Two checks. Additivity: base value + Σ contributions reproduces every raw score, max error ~1e-14 over all 112,540 evaluation pairs. Equality: a unit test checks LightGBM's built-in contributions against the reference shap library. Using the built-in version also keeps shap out of the server image.

Q4Why show reasons against as well as for?

Because the model itself weighs both. For a third of the riskiest pairs, style controls argue for innocence. Hiding that would bias an investigator toward guilt. A fair alert says what looks bad, what argues against it, and what to check.

Q5How are reason tags chosen and ordered?

Each tag is a hand-level behaviour flag. Its lift is how much more often it fires on planted hands than on the pair's other candidate hands. Tags are ordered per scheme by that lift, and tags below ≈ 1.5 are dropped for that scheme. Intuition was wrong here: for isolation, "weak open" (≈ 3.6) beats the defining "open + step aside" (≈ 1.6).

Q6How do the innocent-explanation templates work?

Each of the 8 templates has a trigger feature (for example, "passive player" = a very high fold rate). It fires when the pair's value is in the most extreme 10% of a reference population, stored as percentiles only. The alert shows the most extreme template and its values, or says no common innocent pattern fits. On the website the percentiles are refitted on the simulated card room, so "more extreme than 90%" means the same room.

Q7What did error analysis show?

At the cut where as many pairs are flagged as there are colluders (372): precision = recall = 0.753. All 92 false positives were unlabeled pairs; none of the 1,488 confirmed innocents made the top 372. 37% of the false positives fit no innocent template, so they're good hidden-colluder candidates. Passive players never became false positives, which shows the lift principle working.

Q8What patterns did the missed colluders share?

They were "light": chip-dumping pairs that moved far less (net 94 vs 395 bb/100 for caught pairs), soft-play pairs with almost no heads-up strong check-downs, and pairs with few chances to act ("small sample" 17% vs 7%). That points to detection power as the limit, not a broken feature.

Q9Can SHAP be misleading?

Yes. It explains the model, not the world. Correlated features can split credit unintuitively, and a large attribution isn't proof of collusion. That's why the explanation always comes with evidence hands to read and an innocent story to rule out.

Q10How do you explain the model to a non-technical stakeholder?

Lead with the decision, then a concrete example, then evidence. "This pair is #3 in the queue. Player A folds better hands to B four times as often as to anyone else, and loses 300 bb per 100 hands to B. The best innocent explanation, that A is just weak, doesn't fit, because A's losses to others are normal. Here are the five hands to watch."

Simulator and SimRoom

game/engine.py, bots.py, simroom.py, calibrate.py

The competition's rules forbid publishing its data, so every hand on the website comes from this project's own poker simulator. pokerkit runs the rules, and the project's own bookkeeping writes each hand in the competition's exact schema. Rule-based bots play five styles (tight-aggressive, loose-aggressive, nit, calling station, maniac), plus a tilting decoy and collusion overlays for the three schemes. The SimRoom is a simulated card room: 12 tables × 2,500 hands, 30-bot pools, planted colluders and innocent look-alikes. The Kaggle-trained models score it.

Why it is built this way

  • Publishable by construction. Every simulated hand is ours, with visibly different IDs (SIM-T03, SIM-P0412).
  • Same schema, same code. A 34-invariant conformance test checks every simulated hand against the competition's semantics, and the generator refuses to write hands that fail it.
  • Realistic bots. Bots are calibrated to aggregate competition statistics. All 14 stats fall inside the competition's range, with close medians (VPIP 0.271 vs 0.279, PFR 0.177 vs 0.167).
  • An honest domain-shift test. The collusion is written by me, not by the competition's generator, so the SimRoom measures how well the model transfers.

Top 10 interview questions

Q1Why build a simulator at all?

Three reasons. The rules say the competition data may not be published, so the website needs its own hands. A real visitor would never play enough hands to be scored (evaluation pairs share a median of 76 hands), so bots must fill the table. And because I write the collusion myself, the simulator tests whether the model generalises beyond the generator it was trained on.

Q2Why pokerkit plus your own bookkeeping?

pokerkit handles the rules correctly: turn order, legal raise sizes, side pots, showdown and split pots. But it returns uncalled bets immediately, while the competition keeps them in total_contribution, and fields like amount_to, to_call and pot_before must match the competition exactly. So I keep my own ledger next to pokerkit and cross-check the two after every action.

Q3How do you know simulated hands match the competition schema?

A conformance test of 34 invariants: dtypes, chip conservation, the pot chain, blind positions, the to_call formula, the action vocabulary, showdown flags and no duplicate cards. It passes on random play, bot play, and real competition hands, which tests the tester. The SimRoom generator refuses to write hands that fail.

Q4How do the bots decide?

Each bot sees only its own cards, the board and the betting. Preflop: position-scaled ranges over hand-class percentiles. Postflop: Monte Carlo equity against the remaining opponents, discounted when facing a bet (a bettor's range is stronger), compared with pot odds. Style parameters (looseness, aggression) are jittered per bot.

Q5How did you calibrate the bots?

Against aggregate competition statistics only. The first version reached showdown on 90% of flops vs 26% in the data, because bots compared equity against random cards even when facing a bet. Discounting equity by bet size and widening opening ranges fixed it. Now all 14 statistics are in range, with close medians (WTSD 0.259 vs 0.263, for example).

Q6How is collusion planted, and what is the ground truth?

Each colluding pair gets an overlay for its scheme: soft-play partners avoid betting into each other and join each other's pots; the chip dumper folds strong hands and makes losing calls into the receiver; squeeze partners raise behind each other. Overlays are active only in random windows and in a share of eligible hands (episodic). Every decision an overlay changes is recorded, and those hands are the ground truth.

Q7How well does the Kaggle model transfer to the SimRoom?

SimRoom final 0.465 (Pair AP 0.415, evidence 0.667, behaviour 0.415) vs baselines 0.040 and 0.086. Chip dumping transfers almost perfectly (10 of 11 pairs caught); soft play (2/10) and isolation (2/12) mostly don't. No innocent look-alike reaches the top. Chip dumping is defined by the chips themselves; the model learned the competition generator's version of soft play and isolation, which differs from mine.

Q8Didn't you tune your bots until the detector caught them?

I strengthened the overlays once, for a diagnosed reason: soft-play pairs had changed only 0–8 hands, because they acted only when both happened to be in the same pot. After making each scheme seek its situations, Pair AP rose 0.15 → 0.37. Then I froze the overlays: tuning further against the detector's scores would turn a transfer test into teaching to the test.

Q9What decoys are planted, and do they work?

Tilters (and the aggressive player who sits with them), passive nits paired with maniacs, pairs with identical styles, and pairs seated together unusually often. They're innocent but look suspicious to naive rules. None reached the top K; the best was a seated-together pair at rank 36. The known weakness is different: two calling stations together produce planted-looking hands.

Q10What's the calling-station blind spot, and did you try to fix it?

Two passive callers together check strong hands down and pay each other off, which looks like soft play hand by hand (median hand suspicion 0.35 vs 0.05 for pairs without one). I tried subtracting each player's own average across their other pairs. It scored 0.6429–0.6478 locally vs 0.6510, so not adopted. The issue is the combination of two styles, so the fix would be an expectation for the pair of styles. It's documented as a limitation.

Live scoring and game sessions

scoring.py, game/session.py

scoring.score_tables runs the full Kaggle path on any hands: encode, exact odds, features, hand suspicion, M1/M2/M4, SHAP, tags and innocent templates. A game session is your six-max table with five bots, one of them your secret partner. After every hand, all 15 pairs at the table are rescored, and at the end the session gives a verdict: caught, suspected, got away, or cleared.

Why it is built this way

  • A live table isn't a card room. All six players sit every hand. Scored like the SimRoom, every pair got risk 0.5–0.67, colluding or not.
  • Fix 1, exposure: scale per feature, as if the table were part of a 30-player pool (pair share ÷ 29, player shares ÷ 5).
  • Fix 2, presence: with the partner never absent, "plays differently with the partner seated" just means "loose player", so those features are set to 0 ("no effect"). After both fixes, an honest session's highest risk was 0.002 and a colluding pair stood out at #1 with 0.25.
  • Verdicts need absolute lines, not just rank. With 15 pairs, someone is always top 3.

Top 10 interview questions

Q1Why does the live game need different scoring from the SimRoom?

In the competition a pair shares about 4% of a table's hands and a player plays about 20%. At a live six-max table everyone shares 100%. The model's exposure features were far outside anything it had seen, and every pair scored 0.5–0.67. The fix was to rescale each exposure feature as if the table were part of a 30-player pool.

Q2Why are presence features switched off in the game?

Presence compares play with and without the partner seated. In the game the partner is always seated, so the comparison degenerates into "plays differently from an average player", and loose players looked guilty. Setting them to zero removed the false alarms. The cost: isolation leans on presence, so squeezes are caught less often in the game.

Q3How do you find bugs like these?

By reading the SHAP drivers of obviously wrong scores. An honest session where every pair scored 0.5+ showed exposure features pushing up; after that fix, presence features were the top upward pushes for innocent pairs, at 5–10× SimRoom levels. Each fix was confirmed by an honest control session dropping to near-zero risk.

Q4How is the verdict decided?
  • Caught: your pair is #1 of 15 and risk ≥ 0.093 (the SimRoom break-even risk).
  • Suspected: risk ≥ 0.093, or top 3 and risk ≥ 0.005.
  • Otherwise: "cleared" if you played honestly, "got away" if you were cheating.
Q5Where do the 0.093 and 0.005 thresholds come from?

0.093 is the SimRoom break-even point, where the number of flagged pairs equals the number of truly planted pairs (precision = recall). 0.005 is about the 99th percentile of innocent pairs' risk at 50–100 hands, measured over 40 simulated sessions. Neither was picked by feel.

Q6Tell me about a bug you found by playing your own game.

An honest 50-hand session said "suspected" with risk 0.0005. The rule was "flagged or top 3", and with 15 pairs someone is always top 3, so 17% of innocent pairs were "suspected" by rank alone. Adding the 0.005 line cut that to 0.9% at 50–100 hands. Lesson: a rank is relative, and a verdict needs an absolute threshold too.

Q7How often does the game catch each scheme?

In 80 bot-only sessions at 300 hands ("caught" = #1 and flagged):

  • Chip dumping: 75% / 100% / 100% at 25 / 50 / 100% intensity.
  • Soft play: 0% / 50% / 100%.
  • Isolation: 0% at every intensity (though it ranks #1 in 25–75% of sessions).
  • Honest control: 0%.

Soft play is easier here than in the SimRoom because partners share every hand.

Q8How is rescoring kept fast?

Equity is cached per hand, so a rescore only computes the newest hand. The novelty model is skipped (a percentile over 15 pairs is meaningless). Threads are pinned to one. A rescore takes 0.24–0.57 s on one full CPU core.

Q9How do you stop the page from leaking the bots' cards?

During play the session sends only your own hole cards and cards shown at showdown. The end-of-session report unlocks all cards for the evidence replays. A test checks that bots' cards never appear in the step-by-step events before showdown.

Q10What are the table rules, and why those?

Blinds 1/2, 200-chip stacks (100 big blinds), automatic top-up below 80 chips, button rotating every hand, a 500-hand cap. They match the most common stake and stack depth in the competition data, so the model sees familiar situations.

Game API and hosting

api/main.py, api/Dockerfile, render.yaml

A FastAPI server that runs the game: it creates sessions, applies your move and the bots' replies, auto-plays hands, and returns the detector's ranking, the final report and hand replays. It's packaged in Docker and runs on Render's free tier (512 MB RAM, 0.1 CPU, sleeps after 15 idle minutes). Render deploys it from the repository with a render.yaml Blueprint.

Why it is built this way

  • Python backend. The game must score with the exact same feature code and model files as training.
  • $0 hosting. Hugging Face Docker Spaces became paid-only, so the game moved to Render's free tier, measured first: 228 MB loaded, 286 MB peak over a 500-hand game.
  • Hide the slow part. On 0.1 CPU a rescore takes seconds. So /action returns the moves immediately, scoring runs as a background task while the page animates the hand (10+ seconds), and the page fetches the detector afterwards.
  • No WebSockets. One human per session, with bots acting on the server, so plain request/response is enough.

Top 10 interview questions

Q1Describe the API.

REST under /api:

  • GET /health;
  • create a session (scheme, direction, intensity, partner seat);
  • get its state;
  • post an action;
  • auto-play 1–50 hands;
  • get the detector ranking;
  • get the report;
  • get a hand replay.

Errors use standard codes: 404 unknown or expired session, 409 illegal action, 422 invalid input, 503 table full. Interactive OpenAPI docs are at /docs.

Q2Why FastAPI?

The server must import the same Python feature code and models as training. FastAPI adds request validation from type hints (Pydantic Literal choices and numeric ranges, so a bad request gets a 422 automatically), generated OpenAPI docs, and BackgroundTasks, which made the "score after responding" trick a few lines of code.

Q3How do you keep the game smooth on 0.1 of a CPU?

Measure, then design around it. Moves return at once (median 0.26 s live), and the rescore (~2 s live) runs in a background task while the page animates bots "thinking" for 10+ seconds. When the page asks for the detector, it waits on the same session lock if scoring isn't finished. In practice it answers in 0.43 s after the animation.

Q4How are sessions stored, and what breaks at scale?

In memory, keyed by random UUIDs, with a per-session lock, a 30-minute idle expiry and a cap (5 on the free tier, set by an environment variable). Nothing is persisted and no personal data is collected. With several instances, a request could reach one without your session, so at scale I'd move session state to Redis (or use sticky sessions) and push scoring to a job queue.

Q5Walk me through the Dockerfile.

python:3.13-slim, plus libgomp1 because LightGBM needs OpenMP. A non-root user, then install the package with the game extras, copy the models and API code, and run Uvicorn on $PORT (Render sets it). The image contains only what the server needs: no training data and no notebooks.

Q6Why Render, and what were the alternatives?

The budget was $0. The plan was a Hugging Face Space, but Docker Spaces became paid ($9/month). The options were to pay, wrap the API in a GPU Gradio Space (fragile, and a misuse of the hardware), use Render's free web service, or ship the website only. Render won: Docker, 512 MB is enough (286 MB peak measured), and it deploys straight from the repo with no stored token.

Q7What happens when the server is asleep?

Render's free tier sleeps after 15 idle minutes and takes about a minute to wake. Every page load of the website pings /api/health in the background, so the server is usually awake by the time a visitor opens the game. Meanwhile the page shows a "waking up" screen.

Q8How is the API secured?

CORS allows only the website's origin and localhost. Inputs are validated by Pydantic. The session cap stops memory exhaustion. The container runs as a non-root user. There's no authentication because there's nothing personal to protect: no accounts, no stored data, play chips only.

Q9How is the server deployed?

render.yaml describes the service: Docker, the free plan, a health check on /api/health, and a build filter so only changes to the API, the package, the models or the config trigger a redeploy. Render watches the main branch through its GitHub app, so no deploy secret is stored anywhere.

Q10How did you test the API?

FastAPI's TestClient in pytest: session creation, legal and illegal moves, auto-play, background scoring finishing before /detector answers, the verdict rules, and that bots' cards stay hidden. A load probe measured live latencies after deployment (create 0.41 s, action median 0.26 s, report 0.25 s).

The theory behind it

Collusion detection here is a ranking problem under extreme class imbalance and incomplete labels, solved with gradient-boosted trees on domain features that compare each player with themselves. The theory you need falls into twelve ideas.

Gradient-boosted trees

An ensemble of small decision trees built one after another. Each new tree fits the gradient of the loss of the current ensemble (for log loss, p − y), and is added scaled by a learning rate. LightGBM speeds this up with binned features, leaf-wise growth, gradient-based sampling and feature bundling. Regularise with few leaves, a minimum number of rows per leaf, row and column subsampling, and L2 on leaf values.

Used in: models, evidence

Ranking metrics: AP and MAP@k

Average precision is the mean of the precision at each positive's rank, so it rewards getting positives to the top of the list. A random ranking scores the base rate. Unlike ROC-AUC, it ignores the mass of easy negatives. MAP@k averages AP over the top k results across queries; here, five evidence hands per colluding pair.

Used in: validation

Bayesian shrinkage

With a Beta prior on a rate, the posterior mean is (x + k·base)/(n + k): few observations stay near the prior, many observations follow the data. Taking the prior from the data itself (each player's own rate) is empirical Bayes. It's what keeps a single odd hand from looking like collusion.

Used in: features

Validation and leakage

Cross-validation estimates generalisation only if folds mimic the real split. Group folds keep the same entity (here a table and its players) on one side, and stratification keeps rare classes in every fold. Leakage is any route by which validation sees information the real test won't have: shared entities, the future, or stacked predictions that aren't out of fold.

Used in: validation, evidence

Distribution shift

Covariate shift is when P(x) changes but P(y|x) doesn't. Here, evaluation pairs share fewer hands. Adversarial validation detects it: train a classifier to tell train from test, and a high AUC means shift. Fix it by making features exposure-invariant (rates) and training on data that matches deployment (windows).

Used in: features, live

Learning from positive and unlabeled data

When only some positives are labeled and the rest is "unknown", treating unknown as negative is biased but works if hidden positives are rare. Removing the most positive-looking unknowns ("reliable negatives") reduces the bias. The local metric becomes a lower bound, because hidden positives count as errors.

Used in: models

Multiple-instance learning

Labels come per bag (a pair) while the signal lives in a few instances (planted hands). Score the instances, then pool them (max, mean of the top k) to describe the bag. It avoids averaging the signal away.

Used in: evidence

Calibration

A calibrated model's 0.2 means a 20% chance. Training at an inflated positive rate breaks calibration but not ranking. Platt scaling fits a logistic curve on the scores (2 parameters, robust with few positives); isotonic regression fits a monotone step function (more flexible, needs more data). Neither changes AP.

Used in: models, live

Shapley values and TreeSHAP

The unique fair attribution of a prediction to features: efficiency, symmetry, dummy and additivity. TreeSHAP computes it exactly for tree ensembles in polynomial time. It explains the model, not causality.

Used in: explainability

Anomaly detection

Isolation Forest isolates points with random splits; outliers need fewer splits. It needs no labels, which suits schemes nobody has labeled. Other options are local outlier factor, one-class SVM and autoencoders.

Used in: models

Poker probability: equity and expected value

Equity is the chance of winning the pot from the current state, computed exactly by enumerating the remaining cards or estimated by Monte Carlo sampling (error ∝ 1/√N). A call's EV is equity × (pot + price) − price. Measured at the decision, without future cards, it says whether a choice gave away value.

Used in: hand strength, simulator

Learning to rank

Pointwise: score items independently and sort (what M4 does). Pairwise: learn which of two items should rank higher (RankNet). Listwise: optimise a ranking metric directly (LambdaRank/LambdaMART, approximately NDCG or MAP). Group by query: here, by pair.

Used in: evidence

15 theory questions

Fundamentals behind the components

Q1What is the bias-variance trade-off, and where did it show up here?

Error = bias² + variance + noise. Simple models underfit (high bias); flexible models overfit (high variance). With only 372 positive pairs, variance was the risk, so the models use small trees, subsampling, L2, seed averaging and no hyperparameter search.

Q2How does gradient boosting work?

Start from a constant. Each round, compute every row's gradient of the loss with respect to the current prediction, fit a small tree to the negative gradients, and add it scaled by the learning rate. XGBoost and LightGBM also use the second derivative, so leaf values are Newton steps −G/(H + λ). Small steps with many trees generalise better.

Q3What makes LightGBM different from XGBoost or a random forest?

A random forest averages deep, independent trees on bootstrap samples, which mainly reduces variance. Boosting adds shallow trees sequentially to fix errors, which mainly reduces bias. LightGBM grows trees leaf-wise (best leaf first) on binned features, adds gradient-based sampling and feature bundling, and handles missing values natively. XGBoost grows level by level by default.

Q4Why AP instead of ROC-AUC or accuracy?

With 0.3% positives, "nobody colludes" is 99.7% accurate. ROC-AUC counts plentiful easy negatives and stays high even when the top of the list is full of false alarms. AP focuses on the top of the ranking, which is what an investigator reads, and a random ranking scores the base rate.

Q5What is MAP@k?

For each query, compute the precision at each rank (up to k) where a relevant item appears, average it, and divide by min(number relevant, k). Then average over queries. It rewards putting the relevant items first. Here, the queries are colluding pairs and the items are their five evidence hands.

Q6Explain the Beta-Binomial model behind the shrinkage.

Successes in n trials are Binomial. A Beta(α, β) prior is conjugate, so the posterior is Beta(α + x, β + n − x), with mean (α + x)/(α + β + n). Setting α = k·base and β = k·(1 − base) gives the shrinkage formula, with k acting as the number of pseudo-observations behind the prior.

Q7What's the difference between stratified, group and time-series cross-validation?

Stratified keeps class proportions in every fold. Group keeps all rows of an entity (a user, a patient, a table) in one fold, so the model can't memorise entities. Time-series always trains on the past and validates on the future. This project used stratified + group; the bigger risk was new players, not time.

Q8What is data leakage? Give three examples.

Information in training or validation that won't exist at prediction time, or that ties the two together. Examples: the same player in train and validation folds; a feature computed from future data; stacked predictions that weren't made out of fold. Each makes validation look better than reality.

Q9What is PU learning?

Learning from labeled positives plus an unlabeled pool. Approaches: treat unlabeled as negative (biased; works when hidden positives are rare), find reliable negatives first and then train, or estimate the class prior and reweight. This project used the first two.

Q10What is calibration, and how do you check it?

A model is calibrated if, among cases it scores at p, a fraction p are positive. Check it with a reliability diagram, the Brier score or expected calibration error. Fix it with Platt scaling or isotonic regression. Calibration doesn't change ranking metrics.

Q11What are Shapley values, and why are they trusted for attributions?

They're the average marginal contribution of a feature over all orders of adding features. They're the only attribution satisfying efficiency (contributions sum to the prediction minus the base), symmetry, dummy and additivity. TreeSHAP computes them exactly for trees. Caveat: they describe the model, and correlated features can share credit unintuitively.

Q12How does an Isolation Forest score anomalies?

Random trees split on random features at random thresholds. Anomalies, being few and different, are isolated near the root. The score is based on the average path length across trees, normalised by the expected path length for that sample size. Short paths mean anomalous.

Q13What is Monte Carlo estimation, and what's its error?

Estimating a quantity by averaging random samples, such as equity by dealing random runouts. The standard error shrinks as 1/√N, so 4× the samples halves the error. Use it when exact enumeration is too expensive, and enumerate exactly when the space is small (the turn and river here).

Q14What is covariate shift, and how do you detect it?

The input distribution changes between training and deployment while the input-to-label relationship stays the same. Detect it with adversarial validation (train a classifier to tell the two sets apart; AUC well above 0.5 means shift), population stability indices or per-feature tests. Fix it with invariant features, reweighting, or training data that matches deployment.

Q15Pointwise, pairwise and listwise learning to rank: what's the difference?

Pointwise scores each item independently (classification or regression), then sorts. Pairwise learns which of two items should rank higher. Listwise optimises a ranking metric over the whole list (for example LambdaMART). Pairwise and listwise optimise the order directly, which is why LambdaRank grouped by pair is the next step for evidence ranking here.
